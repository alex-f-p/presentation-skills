# Choosing and pursuing questions

## Branches

Pursue the branches that could change the next thing to be made, and skip the rest. This is not a questionnaire.

| Branch | Decision to expose |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Who determines success, what they already understand, and whose needs come first when they conflict. Infer needs from context, never from job titles or stereotypes. |
| Outcome | What the audience should understand, decide or do, and an observable sign of success. Separate the impression the presenter wants to make from the outcome that is useful to the audience. |
| Evidence | What supports the proposed claim, what remains uncertain, and which objection or alternative could change it. Keep qualifications, and tell illustrations from measurements. |
| Scope and explanation | Which prerequisites, examples and comparisons earn the available time; what to exclude or move into supporting material. |
| Delivery | Live, discussion, recorded or independent reading; time for inspection and interaction; viewing, access, language and distribution needs. |
| Voice | Existing preferences, the speaker’s language and expression worth keeping. Use concrete examples when taste is hard to describe. |

One answer can change several branches. Settle what kind of session it is before discussing slide treatments: a decision meeting may need the recommendation and alternatives early, while a teaching session may need a worked case and practice.

## Each round

1. Name the tension that matters, in a sentence.
2. Ask a small group of questions. Follow the user’s pace, down to one question at a time.
3. When you recommend, give the basis and leave room for a different purpose or priority.

Challenge only real weaknesses. Never invent disagreement to seem rigorous.

Useful challenges:

- “The proposed ending asks for rollout, but the sources cover one demonstration. What evidence would justify rollout, or should the ask be a pilot?”
- “With fifteen minutes including discussion, which outcome should determine what stays: surveying the field or applying one method?”
- “Which reasonable objection could change this recommendation? Does the supplied evidence answer it?”
- “Will recipients need to understand this without the speaker? What explanation must then remain accessible?”

## When an answer is missing

- Look up source questions with the tools you have.
- Keep working on branches that do not depend on the missing fact.
- If the evidence cannot be had, narrow the claim, or record the gap and what it affects.
- Accept “I don’t know”. Do not press the user to guess.

## When talk cannot settle it

Visual fit, pacing and audience understanding may need something to look at or observe. Use existing material or a small trial within scope, state its limits and update the decision it informs. A sample can settle a layout question; it cannot support a claim that lacks evidence. Keep estimated timing, your own reading and observed human response apart.

## The brief

Keep one compact working brief and update it as decisions settle. Reopen an earlier choice only for new evidence or a contradiction. Stop when the next task is defined well enough to start, with any prerequisite that still blocks responsible production named. Certainty on every point is not needed.
