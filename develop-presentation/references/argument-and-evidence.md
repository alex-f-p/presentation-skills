# Argument and evidence

Tie each conclusion to its evidence and state the inference that connects them. When support is weak, verify it, narrow the claim, leave the question open or remove the claim.

## Structure

Choose the structure for the job. A story arc is one option among these.

| Job | Usually needs |
| --- | --- |
| Decision | The recommendation and alternatives early. |
| Mechanism | Prerequisites, then a worked case. |
| Critique | Assumptions and competing approaches. |

## What to keep with a claim

For each important claim, record:

- where in the source it comes from;
- values and units;
- the denominator, baseline and period;
- the conditions of any comparison;
- the uncertainty.

Say which kind of statement it is: measurement, observation, proposal, opinion or invented illustration. Report source conflicts that affect a claim. Check quotations word for word against the source and mark paraphrases.

Keep qualifications that change a claim’s meaning beside the claim; elaboration can go in notes. When asked for scientific justification, read the original research, keep its scope and limits, and separate its findings from advice or preference.

## Scope errors to check

- A faster step is not the same gain for the whole process.
- Percent change, percentage points, rate and elapsed time are different quantities.
- An uncontrolled before-and-after comparison does not show causation.
- One case does not show a typical outcome.
- Missing data is not zero.
- Alternatives need common criteria. Incomplete evidence may support only a conditional choice.

For example, suppose drafting falls from 30 to 12 minutes while approval stays at 60. The total falls from 90 to 72 minutes: 60% less drafting time, but 20% less total time. “60% faster” would be a third claim, about rate.

A scene can show both stages and both totals together while the speaker explains the process. Check that a viewer can tell the drafting reduction from the total reduction. When figures are invented, as these are, say so on the slide.
